II. Chronic v. Acute Pain
	1. The IASP definition of pain is: An unpleasant sensory and emotional experience associated with actual or potential tissue damage, or described in terms of such damage.
	2. Acute pain
		1. sudden injury
		2. sudden illness
		3. must be seen medically, not merely “masked”
		4. usually ends after underlying cause is treated and resolved
	3. Chronic pain
		1. persists for weeks, months and years
		2. also called functional
		3. no recent injury or illness
		4. no serious medical issues
		5. may be from medical issue, but no treatments

III. Pain Theories
	1. Pain Modulation
		1. Nociceptor to Thalamus
		2. Spinal cord to Thalamus
		3. Thalamus to limbic system (emotions), somatosensory cortex (physical sensation) and frontal lobe (thinking)
		4. Signals to spinal cord to moderate the pain
	2. Transmission to CNS
		1. neospinothalamic tract (fast spontaneous pain)
		2. paleospinothalamic tract (slow increasing pain)
		3. A Delta fibers (acute pain) vs C fibers (chronic pain)
			1. interfere with each other
	3. Carr said the classic and clinical view of pain as passive registration of nociception has been updated to include a network of brain structures that employ nociception, other inputs, and memories to actively construct a continually evolving internal model of reality.
	4. Pain Gate
		1. Patrick Wall and Ronald Melzack in 1965
			1. pain is function of balance between large nerve fibers (non-nociceptive signals) and small nerve fibers (nociceptive signals)
				1. if large nerve signals are more than small nerve fiber signals, no pain
					1. concurrent signals, vibration, TENS
				2. if small is more than large, pain
			2. no stimulation means large and small are quiet, gate (interneuron) is closed
			3. stimulation of small fibers means projection neuron is activated and gate is opened
			4. stimulation of large fibers activates projection neuron, but also interneuron, so gate is closed
			5. top down also works
				1. concentrating on something else blocks the pain
				2. brain can also stimulate production of endorphins and enkaphalins, which bind to opioid (morphine) receptor sites and turn off the signal of pain
					1. http://www.nytimes.com/2011/09/14/science/14laughter.html?_r=1 “The
simple muscular exertions involved in producing the familiar ha, ha, ha, [Robin Dunbar, a psychologist at Oxford,] said, trigger an increase in endorphins, the brain chemicals known for their feel-good effect.”
		2. Pain affected by:
			1. Emotional and psychological state
				Stress increases cortisol (which contributes to pain and related symptoms)
				The overlap of pain within emotion circuits suggests it’s a homeostatic emotion like fear, anxiety, or depression, according to Carr.
			2. Memories of past experiences
			3. Upbringing
			4. Attitude
			5. Expectations
			6. Beliefs and values
			7. Relationship of patient and physician
				1. “Current biomedical approaches to understanding the experience of pain need 
to involve a deeper understanding of patient-reported outcomes; clinicians must have a psychosocial understanding of patient and provider responses, as well as objective and experimental assessments, according to Daniel Carr, MD, director, Program on Pain Research, Education and Policy, Professor of Public Health and Community Medicine, Tufts University School of Medicine. “There’s a remarkable convergence going on right now between the two,” Carr said, noting that in pain, patient-reported outcomes and measurable outcomes are like two sides to the looking glass.”
	5. Hilgard and Hilgard's “Hidden Observer” controls pain gate
